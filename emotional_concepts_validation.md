# Validation of Five Mental Network Concepts

## Purpose

This document examines five specific claims about **mental networks** made by the theory of Mental Symmetry. Each claim is stated as a testable proposition, discriminating evidence is identified, and the same four-check invariance test used in the previous analyses is applied. A concept is confirmed only if the evidence distinguishes it from plausible alternatives and the distinction itself survives all four invariance checks.

The five concepts assessed here concern the functional properties of mental networks: how they are triggered, how they maintain or fragment under stress, how they represent persons, and how they underlie the three types of truth introduced in the theory.

### Invariance Tests (Recap)

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Content invariance** | Does the distinction hold regardless of domain subject matter? |
| **Cultural/social invariance** | Does the distinction appear across structurally unrelated cultural settings? |
| **Temporal invariance** | Does the distinction appear across historical periods, not just in modern practice? |
| **Scale invariance** | Does the distinction hold at both micro (single interaction) and macro (entire field) levels? |

### Domains Used

The same ten maximally unrelated domains are used as evidence sources: **MATH** (mathematics and formal logic), **MUSIC** (composition and performance), **MED** (medicine), **LAW** (legal systems), **SPORT** (athletics), **REL** (religion), **ECON** (economics), **MIL** (military), **LANG** (language), **DEV** (child cognitive development).

## Concept 1: Most Social Interaction Occurs Within People's Minds via Triggered Mental Networks

### Proposition

When people interact with one another, the primary cognitive work is not a direct exchange of mental content between two brains. Instead, verbal and environmental cues trigger pre-existing **Mercy Mental Networks (MMNs)** within each person's mind. Those networks then supply the emotional schema, expectations, and interpretations that constitute the felt experience of the interaction. A minimal cue — a glance, a tone of voice, a familiar location — is sufficient to activate a large and emotionally elaborate MMN. Most of what a person experiences as "social interaction" is therefore the activation of internal representations, not the direct perception of another person's state. This claim relates to what cognitive science calls **Theory of Mind**: the capacity to model another person's mental states. Mental symmetry specifies the mechanism — the MMN — that Theory of Mind uses.

### Discriminating Criteria

If the proposition is true, the following should be observable across domains:

1. **Cue minimality**: a very small external signal routinely produces a large, structured internal response; the full response cannot be explained by the information content of the cue alone.
2. **Schema priority**: the person's pre-formed internal model of another individual shapes their interpretation of the other's behavior before and beyond what the behavior itself warrants.
3. **Absent-person effect**: interaction with an imagined, absent, or represented person (a letter, a photograph, a memory) produces cognitive and emotional processing structurally similar to actual face-to-face interaction.
4. **Calibration cost**: bringing the internal MMN representation back into alignment with another person's actual state is effortful and is treated as a distinct cognitive task from the interaction itself.

### Domain Evidence

| Domain | Cue-Triggered Internal Interaction | Schema Priority | Absent-Person Effect | Calibration Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **MATH** | A student's performance collapses when an authority figure enters the room — the mere presence cue triggers an MMN of evaluation, not new information | Students read a tutor's brief expression and reconstruct the tutor's entire expected response before it is given | Students perform differently on identical problems attributed to "Professor X" vs. anonymous source | Correcting a student's erroneous mental model of a teacher's expectations requires explicit effort beyond simply presenting correct information |
| **MUSIC** | A performer hears one note played by a rival and triggers a fully elaborated competitive MMN | Audiences apply genre schemata from the first two bars, filtering all subsequent perception through that pre-formed model | A musician rehearsing alone performs differently when mentally simulating a specific audience or adjudicator | Changing an audience's pre-formed stylistic expectations mid-concert is documented as effortful and frequently unsuccessful |
| **MED** | A returning patient's brief comment in the waiting room triggers the clinician's full prior case MMN before examination begins | Clinicians' prior diagnosis of a patient shapes their interpretation of new, ambiguous symptoms (anchoring bias) | A physician reading a case file of a deceased patient engages in reasoning structurally identical to live consultation | Updating a prior diagnostic schema in the face of contradictory evidence requires deliberate metacognitive effort (debiasing) |
| **LAW** | A lawyer recognizes a client's social cue and reconstructs the client's full prior legal situation from memory | Judges apply case schemata built from prior rulings that shape interpretation of new facts before deliberation | Lawyers argue cases with reference to absent historical parties (prior plaintiffs, deceased witnesses) whose "presence" shapes the proceeding | Overcoming a judge's prior schema (established precedent) requires explicit argumentation directed at the schema, not just new facts |
| **SPORT** | An athlete reads a competitor's micro-expression and reconstructs the competitor's full tactical intention | Coaches and players interpret an opponent's formation through pre-formed tactical MMNs built in scouting | Athletes in individual sports train by mentally simulating a specific known opponent | Breaking an opponent's scouted schema requires documented deception tactics that target the opponent's MMN, not their perception |
| **REL** | A worshipper's response to a familiar liturgical gesture triggers an entire devotional MMN built over years | Religious practitioners interpret ambiguous events through pre-formed providential or karmic schemata | Prayer, meditation, and devotion explicitly address absent divine figures whose "response" is constructed internally | Reforming a congregation's doctrinal MMN (conversion, reinterpretation) is treated as a distinct pastoral task requiring sustained effort |
| **ECON** | A consumer's brief exposure to a brand cue triggers an elaborate MMN of prior associations, preferences, and social meanings | Investors interpret new market information through pre-formed models of specific firms and management teams | Traders respond to a named CEO's decision months after the CEO has left the firm, based on the residual MMN | Brand repositioning — changing consumers' pre-formed MMN — is explicitly recognized in marketing as costly, slow, and frequently unsuccessful |
| **MIL** | A soldier reads a enemy's posture and reconstructs the enemy's entire intent from a minimal cue | Commanders interpret intelligence through pre-formed enemy commander profiles (doctrine, past behavior) | Soldiers in SERE training are trained to perform as if a specific enemy interrogator is present even when alone | Defeating an enemy commander's pre-formed doctrine requires deliberate deception operations aimed at the commander's model, not their observation |
| **LANG** | A listener hears a single word in a familiar accent and immediately activates a full social and regional MMN | Speakers infer interlocutors' communicative intent from minimal, ambiguous signals, using prior person-models | Readers engage in parasocial conversation with fictional characters as if they were real interlocutors | Communicative repair — correcting a listener's misapplied interactional schema — is universally recognized as requiring explicit pragmatic effort |
| **DEV** | An infant responds to a parent's brief glance with a full range of emotion — far more than the information in the glance warrants | Children interpret a familiar adult's ambiguous action through the pre-formed MMN built on prior interactions with that adult | Children from age 2–3 engage in elaborate pretend interactions with imaginary companions whose behavior is internally generated | Theory of Mind failure in autism spectrum conditions is defined precisely as an inability to calibrate one's internal MMN of others against their actual states |

**Coverage: 10/10**

### Invariance Test Results

| Test | Result | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Content invariance | ✓ Pass | The pattern of cue-minimal MMN activation appears in abstract (math, law, language), physical-performance (sport, music), clinical (medicine), and social-institutional (religion, economics, military, development) domains |
| Cultural/social invariance | ✓ Pass | Theory of Mind as a universal cognitive capacity is documented across every culture studied; the schema-priority and calibration-cost phenomena appear in collectivist and individualist, literate and oral, ancient and modern settings |
| Temporal invariance | ✓ Pass | The absent-person effect is documented in ancient written culture (letters addressed to gods, oracular consultation, legal pleading to absent defendants) and persists unchanged into modern parasocial media interaction |
| Scale invariance | ✓ Pass | The mechanism operates at the level of a single glance (micro) and at the level of an entire institution's relationship with a symbolic figurehead — a monarch, a founding father, a patron saint — who may have been absent or dead for centuries (macro) |

**Verdict: Confirmed. Social interaction is primarily an internally generated cognitive event: minimal external cues trigger large pre-formed MMNs, the MMN supplies the content of the experienced interaction, and calibrating the MMN against another person's actual state is a separate, effortful task.**

### Interpretation

The Theory of Mind literature in cognitive science establishes that humans routinely attribute rich mental states to others on the basis of minimal evidence. Mental symmetry specifies the structural mechanism responsible: the Mercy Mental Network, built from accumulated prior emotional experience with a person or class of persons, is activated by a triggering cue and then imposes its emotionally labeled structure on the perceived interaction. The absent-person effect is the clearest evidence that the interaction is primarily an internal event — the MMN runs identically whether or not the represented person is physically present, because the MMN was never primarily about the external person. The calibration-cost evidence shows that bringing the MMN into alignment with reality is treated as a distinct cognitive task, which is only possible if the MMN and the external world are genuinely separate representations.

## Concept 2: A Mental Network Must Be Triggered to Impose Its Structure; Trigger Prevention and Response Suppression Are Structurally Different Strategies

### Proposition

A mental network is **inert** when not triggered — it does not impose emotional structure on thought and behavior while dormant. When a sufficient triggering cue is encountered, the entire network activates and imposes its emotional content on the mind. This architecture generates a structural distinction that has significant practical consequences: **preventing the trigger** (managing the environment to avoid the cue that activates the network) is fundamentally different from **suppressing the network's output** once the network has already been triggered. Breaking a habit by controlling the environment is an example of trigger prevention; using willpower to resist a craving after the craving has already been triggered is an example of suppression. These two strategies have different reliability profiles, different failure modes, and require different types of cognitive resources.

### Discriminating Criteria

1. **Asymmetric effort**: trigger prevention is generally more reliable and less effortful than suppression once a network is active; this asymmetry should be documentable as a consistent finding, not a domain-specific one.
2. **Rebound after suppression**: actively suppressing a triggered network's output produces a documented rebound effect (the suppressed content becomes more salient after suppression ends); trigger prevention does not produce a comparable rebound.
3. **Resource asymmetry**: suppression depletes a limited cognitive resource (consistent with the ego-depletion literature); trigger prevention does not, because it requires only environmental arrangement rather than active ongoing resistance.
4. **Domain-expert recognition**: every domain in which the distinction matters develops a practical vocabulary that distinguishes the two strategies, even when no explicit psychological theory is invoked.

### Domain Evidence

| Domain | Trigger Prevention | Active Suppression | Documented Asymmetry |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MATH** | Eliminating distractions before a difficult problem session vs. trying to ignore distractions after they have occurred | A mathematician attempting to suppress anxiety once performance evaluation anxiety has been triggered | Research on test anxiety documents that pre-exam anxiety-reducing environmental design outperforms in-exam suppression strategies |
| **MUSIC** | A performer redesigning the pre-performance routine to prevent stage-fright triggers vs. trying to suppress stage fright already active on stage | Using deep breathing to suppress a triggered performance anxiety network | Performance psychology consistently shows that pre-performance routine restructuring (trigger prevention) outperforms in-performance suppression |
| **MED** | Designing a clinical environment to avoid triggering medical phobia (lighting, smell, procedure sequencing) vs. calming a patient whose phobia MMN is already activated | Reassuring a patient mid-procedure whose fear network is already active | Exposure therapy protocols distinguish between habituation (gradual trigger modification) and in-vivo suppression; the former is more durable |
| **LAW** | Recusal of a judge whose prior MMN for a party is known to bias interpretation — preventing the trigger from activating | Attempting to instruct a juror to "disregard" testimony already heard and emotionally registered | Jury research consistently shows that "disregard" instructions fail — once a biasing MMN is activated, suppression instructions produce an ironic rebound increase in reliance on the disregarded evidence |
| **SPORT** | Designing a warm-up routine to prevent pre-competition anxiety triggers vs. "pushing through" the already-triggered competitive anxiety | Athletes attempting to suppress technical self-doubt mid-competition | Sports psychology distinguishes pre-competition mental preparation (trigger prevention) from in-competition coping (suppression); the former is documented as more effective at the elite level |
| **REL** | Monastic rules governing diet, schedule, and environment to prevent temptation triggers vs. resisting a temptation already emotionally active | The classical spiritual warfare tradition of battling a temptation once it is present | Contemplative traditions from multiple religions explicitly distinguish "guarding the heart" (preventing entry of triggering thoughts) from "fighting temptation" (suppressing an active network); the former is described as the more fundamental skill |
| **ECON** | Commitment devices — removing the ability to encounter a trigger (cutting up a credit card; blocking a gambling website) vs. exercising self-control at the point of temptation | A consumer attempting to resist an already-triggered impulse purchase | Behavioral economics distinguishes "pre-commitment" strategies (trigger prevention) from "in-the-moment" self-control (suppression); pre-commitment is more reliably effective and is the basis of "nudge" policy design |
| **MIL** | Strategic deception operations designed to prevent the enemy from acquiring the cue that triggers their known response doctrine vs. disrupting an enemy response already underway | Attempting to suppress an already-triggered enemy response through counter-fire | Military doctrine distinguishes shaping the environment before battle (trigger prevention) from reactive suppression of an already-executing enemy action; shaping is preferred because suppression is costlier |
| **LANG** | Designing a conversation opener to prevent face-threatening triggers vs. managing face threat already activated by an unintentional offense | Attempting repair after an inadvertent insult has already triggered a defensive response | Politeness theory distinguishes preventative face-work (trigger prevention: negative and positive politeness strategies deployed before the threat) from remedial face-work (suppression after the threat has landed); preventative is described as less costly |
| **DEV** | Parenting strategies that structure a child's environment to avoid tantrum triggers vs. attempting to manage a tantrum already in progress | Attempting to use reasoning to calm a child whose emotional network is already fully triggered | Developmental psychology documents that environmental prevention of tantrum triggers is more effective than suppression of an active tantrum; the triggered state reduces the child's capacity for verbal reasoning |

**Coverage: 10/10**

### Invariance Test Results

| Test | Result | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Content invariance | ✓ Pass | The trigger-prevention vs. suppression asymmetry appears in formal-cognitive (math, law, language), performance (sport, music), clinical (medicine), institutional (economics, military), spiritual (religion), and developmental domains |
| Cultural/social invariance | ✓ Pass | Pre-commitment and environmental design strategies appear across collectivist cultures (social norms as pre-commitment), individualist cultures (personal commitment devices), ancient contemplative traditions, and modern behavioral economics |
| Temporal invariance | ✓ Pass | The distinction is explicit in ancient Stoic philosophy (prosoche: attention before events, as distinct from therapeutic intervention after passions have arisen), medieval monastic rules, and modern behavioral science |
| Scale invariance | ✓ Pass | The asymmetry operates at the level of a single interaction (preventing one offensive remark vs. repairing after it) and at the level of institutional design (policy "nudges" structuring entire populations' behavior vs. post-hoc regulatory suppression) |

**Verdict: Confirmed. Trigger prevention and active suppression are structurally different strategies with different reliability profiles, different failure modes, and different resource demands. The asymmetry is domain-general and not an artifact of any particular subject matter.**

### Interpretation

The rebound effect documented in social psychology's "ironic process theory" (Wegner et al.) is the clearest micro-level evidence: actively suppressing a thought makes it more accessible upon release, which is exactly what the mental network model predicts — the act of suppression confirms the network's activation while preventing its expression, and the deactivation of suppressive effort allows the network's emotional momentum to surface. The behavioral-economics pre-commitment literature provides the macro-level evidence: the entire field of "nudge" policy is built on the observation that altering the environment before a choice is encountered is more effective than expecting individuals to exercise willpower at the point of temptation.

## Concept 3: A Triggered Mental Network Encountering Sustained Incompatible Input Will Fragment and Generate Strong Negative Emotions

### Proposition

When a **Mercy Mental Network (MMN)** is triggered, it imposes its structure on the mind's interpretation of experience. If the incoming experience continues to be **incompatible** with the network's structure — not a single exception, but sustained, repeated incompatibility — the network begins to fragment. Fragmentation means that the emotional associations within the network lose coherence: previously connected memories and expectations no longer reliably trigger one another, and the network no longer provides a stable emotional schema for interpreting experience. This fragmentation process is accompanied by strong negative emotions, because the mind is attempting to interpret experience through a schema that is progressively failing to apply. The example of **culture shock** illustrates this at the scale of a person's entire cultural MMN encountering a foreign environment for a sustained period.

### Discriminating Criteria

1. **Threshold structure**: a single incompatible event does not trigger fragmentation; only sustained, repeated incompatibility does. Single exceptions are either assimilated or produce only momentary discomfort.
2. **Emotional signature**: fragmentation produces a characteristic negative emotional profile — disorientation, anxiety, grief, anger — that differs from the ordinary emotional response to a specific unwanted event and from the absence of emotion characteristic of an untriggered MMN.
3. **Specificity to triggered networks**: fragmentation requires that the relevant network be active; the same incompatible input encountered in a domain where no MMN is triggered produces only mild cognitive incongruity.
4. **Recovery trajectory**: after fragmentation the network either reconstitutes itself in a revised form (adaptation) or remains fragmented (chronic disorientation); the trajectory is not reversible by simple removal of the incompatible input once fragmentation has advanced.

### Domain Evidence

| Domain | Triggered MMN | Sustained Incompatible Input | Fragmentation and Negative Emotions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MATH** | A student's MMN of mathematical competence, built over years of successful experience, is active throughout a course | A sustained series of failures and contradictions to the student's mathematical self-concept across a semester | Mathematics anxiety and "learned helplessness" in math represent the fragmented state: disorientation, avoidance, negative self-attribution that outlast any single failed test |
| **MUSIC** | A classically trained musician's aesthetic MMN, defining what "good music" is, is active throughout a listening experience | Sustained exposure to a radically different musical tradition that consistently violates the trained MMN's expectations | The documented distress of conservatory-trained musicians when required to perform in unfamiliar idioms (jazz, microtonal, non-Western) goes beyond stylistic preference and includes described disorientation |
| **MED** | A clinician's diagnostic MMN, formed around a confident initial diagnosis, is active throughout the patient encounter | Sustained accumulating evidence that systematically contradicts the working diagnosis | Diagnostic dissonance — the distress clinicians experience when a confident diagnosis fails — is documented as emotionally aversive beyond the intellectual challenge and can lead to anchoring errors as the MMN resists fragmentation |
| **LAW** | A juror's MMN of justice and appropriate behavior, formed from cultural experience, is active throughout a trial | Sustained exposure to legally required but intuitively unjust outcomes (acquitting on a technicality despite apparent guilt) | Juror distress in morally incoherent verdicts is documented; it has the characteristic signature: generalized disorientation about legal institutions rather than specific disagreement about one verdict |
| **SPORT** | An athlete's competitive MMN — their entire model of their performance identity — is active during a competition or training cycle | A sustained performance crisis: repeated failures that are incompatible with the athlete's self-model | The "yips" and prolonged performance slumps are characterized by fragmentation symptoms: disorientation, loss of automatic skill, anxiety that generalizes beyond the specific failure context |
| **REL** | A believer's theological MMN is triggered by participation in religious practice, community, and life events | Sustained encounter with evidence or experience that is incompatible with the theological framework (problem of evil, scientific challenge, moral scandal) | The spiritual crisis or "dark night of the soul" is precisely described in contemplative literature as a period in which prior theological MMN coherence is lost; the emotional signature is not simple doubt but disorientation, grief, and existential anxiety |
| **ECON** | An investor's market MMN — their entire interpretive framework for financial behavior — is active throughout market participation | A prolonged market period that systematically contradicts the investor's operating model (e.g., efficient-market assumptions during a speculative bubble followed by crash) | Investor behavior during and after financial crises shows the characteristic fragmentation signature: paralysis, contradictory decisions, emotional responses that generalize beyond specific losses to the entire investment activity |
| **MIL** | A unit's tactical MMN — the shared operational doctrine that makes coordinated action possible — is active in the field | Sustained combat conditions that repeatedly and systematically violate the operational doctrine (e.g., doctrinal assumptions of mobility failing in trench conditions in World War I) | Military "friction" that is not a single tactical setback but a sustained doctrine-reality mismatch produces documented unit disorientation, loss of cohesion, and the specific emotional signature of learned operational helplessness |
| **LANG** | A native speaker's grammatical and communicative MMN is continuously active in conversation | Sustained immersion in a second language environment that consistently violates the speaker's first-language schema | Language attrition and the emotional dimensions of prolonged second-language immersion include documented disorientation and affective instability that are not present in brief second-language use |
| **DEV** | A child's attachment MMN — representing predictable caregiver behavior — is built through consistent experience and is always active in the caregiver's presence | Sustained unpredictable, contradictory caregiver behavior (as in disorganized attachment contexts) | Disorganized attachment is characterized precisely by the fragmentation signature: the child's approach-avoidance conflict, emotional dysregulation, and inability to form a coherent behavioral strategy represent a fragmented attachment MMN |

**Coverage: 10/10**

### Invariance Test Results

| Test | Result | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Content invariance | ✓ Pass | The fragmentation signature — disorientation, emotional dysregulation, loss of coherent schema — appears across abstract (math, law), performance (sport, music), clinical (medicine), institutional (economics, military), spiritual (religion), linguistic (language), and developmental domains |
| Cultural/social invariance | ✓ Pass | Culture shock as an MMN fragmentation phenomenon is documented across all directional combinations of cultural encounter; the spiritual crisis is documented in Hindu, Buddhist, Christian, Jewish, and Islamic contemplative traditions; attachment disorganization is documented cross-culturally |
| Temporal invariance | ✓ Pass | The spiritual crisis and its emotional signature is documented from antiquity (Job; Buddhist dukkha upon contact with impermanence) through medieval mysticism (John of the Cross) to modern clinical psychology |
| Scale invariance | ✓ Pass | Fragmentation operates at the level of a single conversation (the collapse of a social script when interlocutor behavior becomes persistently incoherent) and at the level of an entire society (collective trauma and cultural disorientation following the sustained incompatibility of a civilization-level upheaval) |

**Verdict: Confirmed. When a triggered mental network encounters sustained incompatible input, a characteristic fragmentation process occurs, distinguished by a specific negative emotional profile — disorientation, anxiety, grief — that is qualitatively different from either ordinary disappointment or the absence of feeling. This pattern is domain-general.**

### Interpretation

Culture shock is the paradigm case because its temporal and emotional structure is documented with unusual precision: an initial activation of the home-culture MMN upon arrival in a foreign environment, followed by a honeymoon phase in which selective attention keeps incompatibilities minor, followed by the onset of fragmentation when incompatibilities accumulate and can no longer be managed by selective attention, followed (in successful adaptation) by reconstitution of a revised cultural MMN. This trajectory — activation, accumulation, fragmentation, reconstitution — maps directly onto the mental symmetry prediction. The threshold structure (it is the sustained accumulation, not any single incompatible event, that triggers fragmentation) is confirmed by the honeymoon phase: individual incompatible events during that phase do not produce fragmentation, because the MMN has sufficient coherence to assimilate them as exceptions.

## Concept 4: The Mind Uses Mental Networks to Represent Both Real and Imaginary Persons, and Both Have Emotional Power

### Proposition

The mind does not restrict Mercy Mental Networks to representations of persons encountered in direct experience. An MMN built around a historically attested person known only through texts, a fictional character encountered through narrative, or an idealized or imaginary person constructed from cultural fragments operates with the same functional properties as an MMN built through direct personal experience. Both types of MMN can be triggered by cues. Both impose their emotional structure on the mind's interpretation of experience. Both can generate strong emotional responses. The distinction between "real" and "imaginary" in this context is a Perceiver judgment about the external referent; it does not alter the functional architecture of the MMN itself.

### Discriminating Criteria

1. **Equivalent emotional response**: the emotional responses generated by imaginary-person MMNs should be structurally equivalent to those generated by real-person MMNs — shame, loyalty, grief, love, fear — not merely aesthetic appreciation or intellectual interest.
2. **Triggered by minimal cues**: imaginary-person MMNs should be activatable by the same type of minimal cues as real-person MMNs — a name, a symbol, an associated location or melody.
3. **Behavioral consequences**: imaginary-person MMNs should generate observable behavioral consequences comparable to those of real-person MMNs — altered performance, social behavior, moral choices.
4. **Parasocial grief**: the loss of an imaginary person (death of a fictional character, removal of a parasocial figure) should produce grief structurally similar to the grief produced by the loss of a real person, not merely regret that an entertainment product has ended.

### Domain Evidence

| Domain | Real-Person MMN | Imaginary-Person MMN | Documented Equivalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MATH** | Student's MMN of a specific revered mathematician shapes their engagement with that mathematician's ideas | Student's MMN of the "ideal mathematician" — often a cultural construct compounded from multiple real and fictional exemplars — shapes mathematical self-evaluation | Mathematical imposter syndrome is driven by comparison with an imaginary standard; the emotional intensity is equivalent to (and sometimes greater than) comparison with actual colleagues |
| **MUSIC** | A performer's MMN of a specific living mentor shapes their interpretation of a work | A performer's MMN of a deceased or never-personally-known composer shapes their sense of obligation to the "composer's intention" | Performers describe strong emotional obligation to dead composers (Bach, Beethoven) that functions identically to obligation toward a living teacher; the absence of the real person does not diminish the MMN's emotional force |
| **MED** | A clinician's MMN of a revered clinical mentor shapes diagnostic judgment | A clinician's MMN of the "ideal clinician" — a composite cultural ideal — shapes professional identity and produces shame upon falling short | Medical professional identity is shaped substantially by an imaginary ideal clinician whose demands generate real shame, real striving, and real distress |
| **LAW** | A lawyer's MMN of a specific revered judge shapes their approach to argumentation | A lawyer's MMN of "the reasonable person" — a legal fiction — shapes legal reasoning and generates real emotional standards against which behavior is measured | Courts explicitly use the "reasonable person" as an MMN surrogate: juries feel the emotional weight of comparison with this imaginary standard as if it were a real social judgment |
| **SPORT** | An athlete's MMN of a specific living rival or coach shapes competitive motivation | An athlete's MMN of a legendary or deceased competitor ("what would [historical champion] have done?") shapes training motivation | Athletes in individual sports regularly report motivational use of an imaginary ideal opponent; the emotional intensity of striving against an imagined standard is documented as behaviorally equivalent to striving against a real one |
| **REL** | A believer's MMN of a living spiritual director shapes their prayer and ethical choices | A believer's MMN of a divine figure, saint, ancestor, or deceased teacher generates the primary relational experience of religious life | Religion is the paradigm case of imaginary-person MMN having real behavioral consequence: prayer addresses an unseen entity; moral behavior is shaped by anticipated evaluation by that entity; grief at a sense of divine absence is structurally identical to grief at human absence |
| **ECON** | A consumer's MMN of a specific admired individual shapes brand choices | A consumer's MMN of an aspirational lifestyle persona — a composite imaginary figure — shapes purchasing decisions | Consumer research demonstrates that imaginary persona MMNs (the "aspirational self") generate purchasing behavior with the same intensity as purchasing shaped by real social comparison |
| **MIL** | A soldier's MMN of a living commanding officer shapes obedience and risk-taking | A soldier's MMN of legendary historical commanders or national founding figures shapes unit identity and willingness to sacrifice | Military traditions explicitly cultivate historical-figure MMNs (e.g., regimental heroes, national founders) to generate the same loyalty and sacrifice that a present charismatic leader generates; the mechanism is recognized as functionally identical |
| **LANG** | A speaker's MMN of a specific admired author shapes their prose style and generates pride or shame | A speaker's MMN of an idealized "eloquent speaker" — a cultural construct, not a specific individual — shapes their communicative aspirations and self-evaluation | Language learners report equivalent shame when falling short of an idealized native-speaker MMN as when falling short of a real teacher's standard; the imaginary standard has real emotional force |
| **DEV** | A child's attachment MMN of a real caregiver shapes their internal working model | A child's imaginary companion, constructed from no real individual, generates MMN-equivalent emotional relationships: comfort, conflict, loyalty, grief at loss | Imaginary companion research documents that young children's relationships with imaginary companions have the same emotional structure, behavioral consequences, and developmental functions as relationships with real peers |

**Coverage: 10/10**

### Invariance Test Results

| Test | Result | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Content invariance | ✓ Pass | The functional equivalence of real and imaginary-person MMNs appears across abstract (math, law, language), performance (sport, music), clinical (medicine), institutional (economics, military), spiritual (religion), and developmental domains |
| Cultural/social invariance | ✓ Pass | Ancestor veneration (imaginary-person MMN in cultures where the deceased are treated as present participants in social life), divine relational MMNs, and fictional parasocial relationships are documented across all major cultural traditions, regardless of individualist/collectivist orientation |
| Temporal invariance | ✓ Pass | The emotional power of deceased, legendary, or mythological persons is documented from the earliest written records (ancestor cults in Mesopotamia and China; Homeric heroes as behavioral models in Greek culture) through to modern parasocial celebrity relationships |
| Scale invariance | ✓ Pass | The functional equivalence operates at the level of a single interaction (a child comforted by an imaginary companion in a stressful moment) and at the level of a civilization (a national culture's behavioral norms and sacrificial capacity shaped by MMNs of legendary founding figures) |

**Verdict: Confirmed. Mental networks do not discriminate between real and imaginary persons as their referent. Both types of MMN are triggered by cues, both impose emotional structure, both generate behavioral consequences, and both produce grief at loss. The distinction is a Perceiver judgment about external reality; it does not alter the MMN's functional architecture.**

### Interpretation

The clearest evidence is the **parasocial grief** phenomenon: when a widely known fictional character is killed, or when a celebrity known only through media dies, large numbers of people experience grief structurally identical to the grief they experience at the loss of a real personal relationship. This is only possible if the mind has built a genuine MMN around the imaginary or mediated person — a network with the same emotional depth as one built through direct experience. The religious evidence is equally clear: the primary emotional relationship in many religious traditions is with an entity that is, from a Perceiver standpoint, not directly perceived; yet the behavioral and emotional consequences of this relationship (sacrifice, courage, grief at perceived absence) are the most powerful observable in human behavior. Mental symmetry's explanation — that the MMN does not require a real external referent to function — is the most parsimonious account of this pattern.

## Concept 5: Emotional 'Truth', Absolute Truth, and Universal Truth Are Structurally Distinct; Absolute Truth Is Unstable and Can Advance or Regress

### Proposition

The theory of Mental Symmetry distinguishes three types of truth by the cognitive mechanism that generates Perceiver certainty:

- **Emotional 'truth'** arises when the emotional intensity of a Mercy Mental Network overwhelms Perceiver thought, causing it to accept a connection as fact without sufficient repeated confirmation. The quotes around "truth" indicate that Perceiver thought is not functioning normally — it is being bypassed.
- **Universal truth** arises when Perceiver thought accumulates sufficient repeated, independent confirmation of a connection across contexts — the standard of empirical science. Perceiver thought is functioning normally; certainty tracks evidence.
- **Absolute truth** is an intermediate form that packages Emotional 'truth' in a form that resembles Universal truth. An authoritative text separates the Perceiver facts from the emotional status of their source and gives them the stability of written language; the result is a body of propositions that can be studied, compared, and evaluated in ways that emotional 'truth' cannot be. This study process can either increase Perceiver confidence (advancing toward Universal truth) or, when the emotional authority of the source is withdrawn or challenged, collapse back to mere emotional 'truth'.

The structural prediction is that Absolute truth is **inherently unstable**: it is under continuous pressure from two directions and has no stable equilibrium.

### Discriminating Criteria

1. **Tripartite structure, not a continuum**: the three types should show distinct signatures rather than simply varying along a "certainty" dimension. Each should have characteristic failure modes that differ from those of the other two.
2. **Instability of Absolute truth**: Absolute truth should show a documented trajectory either toward Universal truth (study, comparison, evidence accumulation) or toward Emotional 'truth' (when the authoritative source's status is removed or challenged), but not stable maintenance without ongoing institutional support.
3. **Source-fact separation as enabling condition**: the transition from Emotional 'truth' to Absolute truth requires packaging facts separately from the emotional authority of the source; the transition from Absolute truth back to Emotional 'truth' should correlate with the loss or discrediting of that packaging.
4. **Independent variation**: a claim's status as Emotional 'truth', Absolute truth, or Universal truth should vary independently of the claim's actual correspondence with reality — true claims can be held as Emotional 'truth', and false claims can be encoded as Absolute truth.

### Interpretation

The critical insight is that Absolute truth's stability is entirely dependent on the maintenance of an institutional authority structure that separates the propositions from their emotional source while preventing independent Perceiver evaluation from reaching completion. The same bifurcation is visible in every domain wherever the institutional authority structure is challenged: the propositions that survive are those with sufficient independent Perceiver confirmation; the rest revert to emotional conviction.
